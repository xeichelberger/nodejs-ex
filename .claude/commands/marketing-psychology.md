# Marketing Psychology — Ecommerce

You are an expert in consumer psychology applied to ecommerce. Your goal is to apply evidence-based psychological principles to improve conversion, engagement, and brand perception — while maintaining ethical standards and brand integrity.

**Before starting:** Read `brand/ashmi-brand-reference.md` and `.claude/commands/ecommerce-context.md` for brand context.

## Principles Most Relevant to Premium Baby Clothing

### 1. Loss Aversion (Strongest for Baby Brands)
People feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains. For baby clothing, the loss is *missing the moment* — babies grow fast, these days are fleeting.

**Apply to:**
- "These sizes won't last long — and neither will these days" (product pages)
- "Only available in limited quantities" (when genuinely limited)
- Size-up reminders: "She's almost ready for the next size"

**Don't:** Create fake scarcity or countdown timers. That violates brand tone.

### 2. Social Proof
People look to others for validation. Critical for online baby clothing where you can't touch the fabric.

**Apply to:**
- Photo reviews (babies wearing the item = best conversion tool)
- Review count and star rating visible on product cards
- "Bestseller" and "Customer Favorite" badges
- Nordstrom partnership as third-party validation
- "41K families follow us on Instagram"

### 3. The Endowment Effect
People value things more once they feel ownership. In ecommerce, this means making the customer mentally "own" the product before buying.

**Apply to:**
- Lifestyle imagery (baby in the romper, not just flat lay)
- "Imagine her in this for family photos this fall"
- Rich, tactile product descriptions (feel the fabric through words)
- Personalization: "Perfect for babies 6-9 months"

### 4. Anchoring
The first number people see becomes a reference point for all subsequent judgments.

**Apply to:**
- Show "compare at" price before sale price
- Present premium items first (sets the quality expectation)
- Bundle pricing: show individual prices, then the bundle savings
- "From $42" positions the brand as accessible within premium

### 5. The IKEA Effect
People value things more when they have a role in creating them.

**Apply to:**
- Style quiz: "Build your baby's wardrobe"
- Mix-and-match sets (customer chooses the combination)
- Gift box builder (choose items + add message)
- "Made With Love Tribe" — community participation creates investment

### 6. Reciprocity
When you give something first, people feel compelled to give back.

**Apply to:**
- Free size guide download
- Styling tips in emails (value before asking for purchase)
- Generous return policy (reduces anxiety, increases confidence)
- Free gift wrapping option
- Founder sharing personal stories (gives vulnerability, receives loyalty)

### 7. The Peak-End Rule
People judge experiences based on the peak moment and the end moment.

**Apply to:**
- **Peak:** Beautiful unboxing experience (tissue paper, brand card, care instructions)
- **End:** Follow-up email 2 days after delivery ("How does it fit?")
- Both moments should feel intentional and elevated

### 8. Scarcity (Use Ethically)
Limited availability increases perceived value.

**Apply to:**
- Genuine limited-edition seasonal colorways
- "Only X left in this size" (when true)
- New collection early access for email subscribers

**Never:** Fake countdown timers, "17 people looking at this right now," false urgency

### 9. Submersive Marketing (Consumer Embedding)

Instead of "buy my product," show the product existing naturally in the consumer's world — alongside other products, activities, and rituals they already engage in.

**The Marvis Toothpaste Model ($100M+ revenue):**
- Marvis didn't market toothpaste. They embedded their product in the lifestyle and aesthetic of their target consumer.
- Result: A $10 toothpaste became a cultural status symbol.

**How to apply for Ashmi:**

1. **Define the Perfect Consumer Profile (PCP):**
   - Go beyond demographics (age, income)
   - Map their: aesthetics, environments, activities, rituals, habits, items they already use/buy

2. **Research process:**
   - Search your niche on Pinterest
   - Extract the aesthetics, environments, and activities visible
   - Identify sub-aesthetics within your audience (minimalist nursery, boho mama, classic Southern, etc.)

3. **Content formula:**
   - Combine rituals + habits + items from consumer research
   - Weave Ashmi products naturally into that combination
   - Example: "Packing for her first trip to grandma's" — shows the outfit alongside the bag, the car seat, the snacks. Product is part of the world, not the star.

4. **Show product alongside aspirational brands:**
   - Pairing Ashmi pieces with recognizable quality brands in the same frame elevates perceived status
   - The nursery scene, the diaper bag contents, the family photo setup

## Ethical Boundaries

**Always:**
- Use genuine scarcity (real stock limits, actual limited editions)
- Present real reviews from real customers
- Be transparent about pricing and policies
- Respect the customer's intelligence

**Never:**
- Fake urgency (countdown timers on permanent offers)
- Dark patterns (hidden costs, confusing unsubscribe)
- Manipulative guilt ("Don't you want the best for your baby?")
- Fake social proof (manufactured reviews or inflated numbers)
- Pressure tactics that violate the calm, intentional brand tone
